# The Chabad-Lubavitch Dynasty: An Overview

## What is Chabad-Lubavitch?

Chabad-Lubavitch is one of the largest and most influential branches of Hasidic Judaism. It was founded in the late 18th century by Rabbi Schneur Zalman of Liadi (the "Alter Rebbe") in what is now Belarus. The movement takes its two names from its core ideas and its geography:

- **Chabad** (חב"ד) is a Hebrew acronym for *Chochmah*, *Binah*, *Daas* — "Wisdom, Understanding, Knowledge" — the three intellectual faculties of the soul. The name signals the movement's defining emphasis: that the heart's service of God should flow from disciplined, sustained *intellectual* contemplation of the Divine, rather than from raw emotion alone. This is what set Chabad apart from other early Hasidic schools.
- **Lubavitch** ("town of love" / Lyubavichi) is the small town in Russia that served as the movement's headquarters for roughly a century (c. 1813–1915), under the third through fifth Rebbes. The name stuck even after the center of the movement moved to Rostov, Riga, Warsaw, and finally Brooklyn, New York (770 Eastern Parkway).

### What "the seven Rebbes" means

The leadership of Chabad-Lubavitch passed through **seven** successive Rebbes (Hebrew: *Admorim*, sing. *Admur*; from *Adoneinu Moreinu v'Rabbeinu*, "our master, teacher, and rabbi") in **a single, unbroken chain from 1745 to 1994** — that is, from the birth of the first Rebbe to the passing of the seventh. Each Rebbe was the direct successor of the previous one, and from the third Rebbe onward all were patrilineal descendants of the founder (the family name **Schneersohn / Schneerson** derives from the founder's name, Schneur). The chain ran father-to-son in most generations, with two exceptions noted in the table below.

After the passing of the seventh Rebbe in 1994, no successor was appointed, and the movement has continued without an eighth Rebbe. When Chabad chassidim say "**the** Rebbe" without qualification, they almost always mean the seventh.

## The Seven Rebbes

| # | Common name / title | Full name | Life years | Years of leadership | Relationship to predecessor |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Alter Rebbe** ("Old Rebbe"); *Baal HaTanya*; *Admur HaZaken* | Rabbi Schneur Zalman of Liadi | 1745–1812 | c. 1788–1812 | Founder of Chabad; principal disciple of the Maggid of Mezeritch |
| 2 | **Mitteler Rebbe** ("Middle Rebbe"); *Admur HaEmtzaei* | Rabbi DovBer Schneuri | 1773–1827 | 1812–1827 | Son of the Alter Rebbe |
| 3 | **Tzemach Tzedek** ("Righteous Scion") | Rabbi Menachem Mendel Schneersohn | 1789–1866 | 1831–1866 | Nephew **and** son-in-law of the Mitteler Rebbe; orphaned grandson of the Alter Rebbe (raised by him) |
| 4 | **Rebbe Maharash** (acronym: Moreinu HaRav Shmuel) | Rabbi Shmuel Schneersohn | 1834–1882 | 1866–1882 | Youngest son of the Tzemach Tzedek |
| 5 | **Rebbe Rashab** (acronym: Rabbi Shalom Ber) | Rabbi Shalom DovBer Schneersohn | 1860–1920 | 1882/1893–1920 | Son of the Rebbe Maharash |
| 6 | **Rebbe Rayatz / Frierdiker (Previous) Rebbe** (acronym: Rabbi Yosef Yitzchak) | Rabbi Yosef Yitzchak Schneersohn | 1880–1950 | 1920–1950 | Only son of the Rebbe Rashab |
| 7 | **The Rebbe** (acronym: Ramash) | Rabbi Menachem Mendel Schneerson | 1902–1994 | 1950/1951–1994 | Son-in-law of the Rebbe Rayatz (and a great-great-grandson of the Tzemach Tzedek) |

**Notes on the table:**
- The first Rebbe's leadership is dated from when he assumed independent communal leadership after the Maggid's passing (1772) and the publication/maturation of his movement; his most active leadership of an organized Chabad following is generally placed c. 1788 onward.
- The **Tzemach Tzedek** broke the strict father-to-son line: he was the Mitteler Rebbe's nephew and son-in-law, and the Alter Rebbe's grandson (raised in the Alter Rebbe's home after his mother died). His leadership began only c. 1831, after a roughly three-year period in which he declined the role before accepting it.
- The **Rebbe Rashab** assumed full leadership gradually after his father's death in 1882, formally accepting the mantle around 1893.
- The **seventh Rebbe** accepted leadership a full year after his predecessor's passing, formally on Yud Shvat 5711 (January 1951), having spent 1950 declining the role.

## Timeline

A master timeline of births, successions, and passings across the dynasty (1745–1994):

- **1745** — Birth of Rabbi Schneur Zalman, the Alter Rebbe (1st).
- **1772** — Passing of the Maggid of Mezeritch; the Alter Rebbe begins to emerge as a leader.
- **1773** — Birth of Rabbi DovBer, the Mitteler Rebbe (2nd).
- **1789** — Birth of Rabbi Menachem Mendel, the Tzemach Tzedek (3rd).
- **1797** — First printing of the *Tanya*, the Alter Rebbe's foundational work.
- **1812** — Passing of the Alter Rebbe (during the flight from Napoleon's invasion); succession of the Mitteler Rebbe (2nd).
- **1813** — The Mitteler Rebbe settles in the town of **Lubavitch**, which becomes the movement's center.
- **1827** — Passing of the Mitteler Rebbe.
- **1831** — Succession of the Tzemach Tzedek (3rd), after an interregnum.
- **1834** — Birth of Rabbi Shmuel, the Rebbe Maharash (4th).
- **1860** — Birth of Rabbi Shalom DovBer, the Rebbe Rashab (5th).
- **1866** — Passing of the Tzemach Tzedek; succession of the Rebbe Maharash (4th).
- **1880** — Birth of Rabbi Yosef Yitzchak, the Rebbe Rayatz (6th).
- **1882** — Passing of the Rebbe Maharash; the Rebbe Rashab (5th) begins to lead.
- **1893** — The Rebbe Rashab formally accepts the full leadership.
- **1897** — The Rebbe Rashab founds the **Tomchei Temimim** yeshivah in Lubavitch.
- **1902** — Birth of Rabbi Menachem Mendel Schneerson, the seventh Rebbe.
- **1915** — Amid World War I, the movement's center leaves the town of Lubavitch (to Rostov-on-Don).
- **1920** — Passing of the Rebbe Rashab; succession of the Rebbe Rayatz (6th).
- **1927** — The Rebbe Rayatz is arrested by the Soviets, sentenced, then released and exiled; he leaves the USSR (settling in Riga, then Warsaw).
- **1940** — The Rebbe Rayatz arrives in the United States, establishing headquarters at **770 Eastern Parkway**, Brooklyn.
- **1950** — Passing of the Rebbe Rayatz (10 Shvat / Yud Shvat).
- **1951** — On Yud Shvat 5711, Rabbi Menachem Mendel Schneerson formally accepts the leadership as the seventh Rebbe.
- **1994** — Passing of the seventh Rebbe (3 Tammuz / Gimmel Tammuz). No successor is appointed.

## The Major Collected Series

Chabad's literature has been gathered, edited, and published over more than a century, principally by **Kehot Publication Society** (Otzar HaChassidim), the movement's official publishing house, founded by the sixth Rebbe in 1942. The corpus is organized partly **by Rebbe** and partly **by genre**, and the same teaching can appear in more than one series. Here is how to read the big cross-Rebbe series:

**Discourse (maamar) collections, by Rebbe — chronologically:**

- **Maamarei Admur HaZaken** — The collected chassidic discourses (*maamarim*) of the **1st Rebbe** (the Alter Rebbe). Published by Kehot in many volumes, organized by year and by topic (e.g., volumes on the festivals, on Tanach, "Inyanim," "Hanachos," etc.). His best-known discourse collections also circulate as the classics **Torah Or** and **Likkutei Torah**.
- **Maamarei Admur HaEmtzaei** — The collected discourses of the **2nd Rebbe** (the Mitteler Rebbe), known for unusually long, systematic, expansive discourses. Sorted into volumes by subject (e.g., *Bamidbar*, *Devarim*, festivals, "Kuntreisim," "Inyanim"). His signature standalone works include **Toras Chaim** and **Shaar HaYichud**.
- **Or HaTorah** (also spelled *Ohr HaTorah*) — The vast running commentary/discourse corpus of the **3rd Rebbe** (the Tzemach Tzedek), arranged following the order of the Torah and the calendar (Bereishis through Devarim, then festivals, Tehillim, Nach, etc.). It is enormous (tens of volumes) and is essentially his lifelong notebooks of chassidic exposition. His standalone classic is **Derech Mitzvosecha** (*Sefer HaMitzvos / Taamei HaMitzvos*).
- **Toras Shmuel** — The discourses of the **4th Rebbe** (the Rebbe Maharash), published by year (e.g., the volume named for the Hebrew year in which the discourses were said — *5626*, *5627*, *5633*, etc.). The Maharash pioneered the *hemshech* — a continuous, themed series of discourses delivered over weeks or months.

**Sefer HaMaamarim ("Book of Discourses"), by year:**

- **Sefer HaMaamarim** is the standard title for the discourse collections of the **5th, 6th, and 7th Rebbes**, organized **by Hebrew year**. So you will see *Sefer HaMaamarim 5666*, *5672*, *5700*, *5710*, *Melukat*, etc.
  - For the **Rashab (5th)**, the most famous are the great *hemsheichim*: **Hemshech Samech Vov** (the series begun in the year 5666 / 1905–06) and **Hemshech Ayin Beis** (begun 5672 / 1912, the monumental multi-year "Yom Tov Shel Rosh Hashanah 5666"... actually titled *BeShaah SheHikdimu 5672*).
  - For the **Rayatz (6th)** and **the Rebbe (7th)**, *Sefer HaMaamarim* volumes are likewise filed by year. The Rebbe's own re-edited discourses are gathered as **Sefer HaMaamarim Melukat** ("Selected Discourses").

**Talks, letters, and cross-cutting series (mostly 6th and 7th Rebbes):**

- **Igros Kodesh** ("Holy Letters") — Collected **correspondence**. Published for several Rebbes (the Alter Rebbe through the Rebbe), but the great multi-volume sets are those of the **6th Rebbe** (Rayatz) and the **7th Rebbe** — the latter running to 30+ volumes of letters on Torah, communal affairs, and personal guidance.
- **Toras Menachem – Hisvaaduyos** ("Teachings of Menachem – Gatherings") — The near-**verbatim transcripts** of the seventh Rebbe's *farbrengens* (public chassidic gatherings), arranged **chronologically by date**. This is the rawest, most complete record of his spoken talks. (An earlier sub-series for his first years is **Toras Menachem – Hisvaaduyos 5710–5714** and onward; *Toras Menachem – Sefer HaMaamarim* and *– Reshimas HaYoman* are companion sets.)
- **Sefer HaSichos** ("Book of Talks") — **Edited** talks (*sichos*), as opposed to the verbatim Hisvaaduyos. Published for the 6th Rebbe (by year) and the 7th Rebbe (the later edited annual volumes, e.g., *Sefer HaSichos 5748*–*5752*).
- **Likkutei Sichos** ("Collected Talks") — The seventh Rebbe's flagship work: 39 volumes of **talks he personally edited into written essays**, organized by the weekly Torah portion and the festivals. The most studied and most translated of all his works.
- **Likkutei Dibburim** ("Collected Discourses/Talks") — The edited talks of the **6th Rebbe** (Rayatz), rich in chassidic history, stories of the earlier Rebbes, and narrative teaching.
- **Reshimos** ("Notes") — Private **notebooks/jottings of the seventh Rebbe**, discovered and published after his passing (from the 1990s onward); they contain discourses, insights, diary-like entries, and accounts of the previous Rebbes.
- **Hayom Yom** ("From Day to Day") — A **daily anthology**: one short chassidic thought (drawn from the Rebbeim, especially the Rayatz) for every day of the Jewish year, compiled by the seventh Rebbe in 1942–43 at his predecessor's request. The standard "entry point" to the whole corpus.

**How to read them, in short:**

- To study a Rebbe's *discourses* (maamarim): go to his named collection (Maamarei Admur HaZaken/HaEmtzaei, Or HaTorah, Toras Shmuel) or his *Sefer HaMaamarim* by year (Rashab, Rayatz, Rebbe).
- To study the seventh Rebbe's *talks*: start with **Likkutei Sichos** (edited, by parsha), then **Sefer HaSichos** (edited, by year), then **Toras Menachem – Hisvaaduyos** (verbatim, by date).
- For *letters*: **Igros Kodesh**.
- For *history, stories, and a gentle entry*: **Likkutei Dibburim**, **Hayom Yom**, and **Reshimos**.

## Genres of Chabad Literature

Reading Chabad sources requires knowing which **genre** a text belongs to, because genre determines its style, authority, and how it is studied:

- **Maamar (מאמר; pl. *maamarim*) — chassidic discourse.** The central genre. A maamar is a formal, often abstract exposition of a chassidic concept, typically delivered orally by a Rebbe (traditionally in a distinctive sing-song chant, eyes closed) and later transcribed. Maamarim are usually built on a verse or rabbinic saying and develop deep ideas in Divine service, the soul, and the metaphysics of creation. They are considered the Rebbe's most exalted, "drawn-from-above" teaching. (Collected in Maamarei Admur HaZaken, Or HaTorah, Toras Shmuel, Sefer HaMaamarim, etc.)
- **Sicha (שיחה; pl. *sichos*) — talk.** A more conversational, applied address, usually given at a *farbrengen*. Sichos range over Torah insights, current events, communal directives, and practical guidance, and are generally more accessible than maamarim. The seventh Rebbe elevated the sicha into a rigorous analytical genre (see Likkutei Sichos). (Collected in Likkutei Sichos, Sefer HaSichos, Toras Menachem, Likkutei Dibburim.)
- **Igros (אגרות) — letters.** Written correspondence from a Rebbe to individuals or communities: halachic and chassidic guidance, blessings, personal counsel, and administrative direction. (Collected in Igros Kodesh.)
- **Nigleh / Halacha — the "revealed" Torah.** As opposed to the esoteric/"hidden" (nistar) teachings, this is the classical exoteric Torah: Talmud, codes, and practical Jewish law. The Alter Rebbe is monumental here for his **Shulchan Aruch HaRav** (a re-codification of Jewish law) and his **Siddur** (prayer book with laws); the Tzemach Tzedek for his halachic responsa (**Tzemach Tzedek She'eilos U'Teshuvos**) and his **Tzemach Tzedek** on Talmud. Several Rebbes were major *nigleh* scholars in addition to chassidic masters.
- **Kabbalah / Biurei Zohar — esoteric and Zohar commentary.** Chassidus is rooted in Kabbalah (Lurianic Kabbalah, the Zohar). A distinct stream of Chabad literature explicitly explains kabbalistic texts and the **Zohar** in chassidic terms — e.g., **Biurei HaZohar** by the Mitteler Rebbe and by the Tzemach Tzedek. These works bridge raw Kabbalah and the contemplative chassidic system of Chabad.

In practice the genres interlock: a sicha may quote a maamar; a maamar may unpack a Zohar passage; the Alter Rebbe's halachic and chassidic writings are studied as one organic system.

## What "everything ever written or said" realistically means

It is tempting to imagine a single shelf holding the complete output of the seven Rebbes. In reality the corpus is **enormous, uneven, and only partly accessible**, and a few honest caveats are essential:

- **The scale is staggering.** The Tzemach Tzedek's writings alone are estimated in the hundreds of thousands of manuscript pages. The seventh Rebbe spoke for an estimated 11,000+ hours in public, produced 200+ volumes of talks and over a thousand published maamarim, and wrote tens of thousands of letters. No individual could read all of it in a lifetime.
- **Most of the *spoken* material survives only as transcription, not as recordings or autographs.** For the first six Rebbes there are no audio recordings at all; their maamarim and sichos reach us only because students wrote down *hanachos* (transcribed notes) — often reconstructed from memory after the fact, sometimes years later, and of varying reliability. Even for the seventh Rebbe, the talks exist in two tiers: the **edited** texts he reviewed himself (highest authority — Likkutei Sichos, edited Sefer HaSichos, Sefer HaMaamarim Melukat) versus the far larger body of **unedited** transcripts (Toras Menachem – Hisvaaduyos) taken down by listeners. The distinction matters when citing.
- **Much remains unpublished or only recently published.** Manuscripts continue to surface and be edited; the **Reshimos** notebooks, for instance, were only published from the 1990s. Some material exists only in private archives (notably the contested Chabad library / *Schneerson Collection*).
- **Much is under copyright.** Kehot Publication Society holds rights to the modern critical editions, translations, and many of the collected series. Full free reproduction of those texts is restricted, even where the underlying teaching is centuries old. This shapes what is legally available online versus in print.
